Ants: the 150 years old success story

CHENNAI: A fuzzy black dot scurries around Aravind Selvam’s feet, running frantically, its movements blurry and its identity hidden from most. “That’s the Velvet Sugar Ant,” he exclaims softly, as he crouches down next to it, fixes his glasses, and says, “Jomi, come here!” Selvam and Jomi Jose from Palluyir are not experts on ants but their passion for nature brings them to the Egmore museum on a Tuesday afternoon to look through the exhibits and observe the ants that roam the museum gardens.

For 150 million years ants have thrived in a world where many organisms failed to survive the arduous journey of evolution. Their success and efficiency draws not only passionate nature enthusiasts like Jose and Selvam, but also inspires research in experimental biology and theoretical physics. While pet ants are in demand in certain parts of the world, with queen ants selling for almost Rs 3000 on Amazon, most people are hardly familiar with these creatures.

People assume that ants are just black or red, sometimes harmless, and ocassionally painful. However, unlike common misconceptions, over 12500 species of ants have been identified worldwide. While they scurry around nameless to most, a small Chennai-based team of nature lovers, part of an organisation called Palluyir, has recently compiled a guidebook on ants. The guidebook transforms these tiny, jittery creatures into identifiable individuals with names and distinct characteristics.

Crouching next to a few Velvet Sugar Ants, Selvam explains, “Their body has four parts: the head, this middle section that we call the mesosoma, the last section also called the gastor, and petioles which connect the mesosoma to the gastor.” The Velvet Sugar has a reddish brown body with a distinct grey tinge on its gastor. Its characteristic gait, a series of jittery spurts with quick short breaks, allows Selvam to spot it even from a distance. Barely a meter away, scurrying along a rusty brick wall is another ant species with a similar gait and antennae almost the size of its body, earning it the name ‘Longhorn Crazy Ant’.

As the duo strolls through the Egmore museum gardens, along the crevices on the bark of a softwood tree, they spot the hurried movements of the Arboreal Bicoloured Ant known for its painful bite that can cause severe allergic reactions among mammals. Nearby, they also spot tiny red Destructor Ants along a thin green stem. Pointing to a line of scurrying red dots, Selvam whispers, “Just like how humans rear cows and then take their milk, these Destructor Ants have domesticated the mealybug. It produces a sweet transparent droplet of nectar every few minutes, and the ants just come and slurp it up!”

When Jose and Selvam take school children for nature-education activities, he asks the children to observe and then identify parts of the ants and describe them in writing. “The best part is when they get excited to show me what they have written,” he recalls fondly. In those few sessions, Jose and Selvam witness a change in the behaviour of children towards ants. “I’ve seen empathy form within a few sessions,” Selvam remembers how children intervene when a fellow classmate disturbs an anthill, “I have seen kids say ‘Listen, if someone destroys your home, would you be okay with that?”

As Jose and Selvam explore the Egmore Museum, they find about seven to eight species of ants within a radius of about 20 metres. They don’t touch them, they don’t disturb them, they make sure to not disrupt the ant trails. Over 1500 kms away, in Kolkata, at the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research, Annagiri Sumana too, a professor in the department of biological sciences, scours empty plots of land with a team of researchers to observe these creatures. However, unlike Jose and Selvam’s passion driven modules, Sumana’s research demands scientific rigour. Sometimes, she packs the little ants and carries them back to her laboratory where she marks them with different colours, and releases them into a controlled environment where she can observe their behaviour and problem solving skills.

“My team and I have spent the past decade studying three things about ants: their history, their reproduction, and their methods of relocation,” Sumana explains. Relocation is an activity that all humans are familiar with, “But we can pack up our luggage into cardboard boxes and get it shipped to a new location, but ants can’t,” she says. They must travel significant distances, carrying extremely fragile luggage, their larva, their eggs, and their pupa, from which future generations of ants shall emerge.

About 15 years ago, Sumana was observing an ant called the Diacamma indicum when she noticed their peculiar way of transporting themselves from one place to another. Unlike some ant species that make trails and follow one another, the Diacamma indicum relocates using a technique known as tandem running. In their colony of ants, there are a few ants, the leaders, that guide others one at a time, from one location to another. “I accidentally saw this happening, and I thought it was a huge waste of time and effort,” Sumana explains, “Ants are known to make trails, so why would they just take one animal at a time?” Her fascination with the phenomenon of tandem running led her to many other research questions.

Sumana noticed how the path taken during tandem running is extremely efficient. “We would take one ant and drop it off in a completely unknown territory and we would observe how it moves around and finds its way back,” she says and adds, “Every pebble and blade of grass is an obstacle for these animals, but despite that they show about 85% efficiency in navigating fields which are completely unknown.”

In her laboratory, Sumana creates experiments where she observes, among other things, how ants choose the more efficient paths when offered multiple choices. Even when stripped of their capacity to see and smell, ants maneouver themselves with remarkable efficiency using path integration, a method by which they track their own movement. “Ants are known to calculate how many steps they take and in which direction,” Sumana explains. Based on that information, they can then reverse engineer the path they have taken to return to where they began.

“I have worked with eight PhD students who have studied ant relocation, and just looking at tandem running leads to so many questions, but,” Sumana sighs, “In the entire country, the number of people who work with ants is less than what you can count on one hand!” Yet, while Sumana conducts her experiments, Dr. Shakti N Menon, another researcher who shares her fascination with ants, sips on his daily 11 am coffee over conversations with collegues at the Institute of Mathematical Sciences (IMSc) in Chennai.

Menon, a senior project scientist at IMSc does not go foraging for ants in his vicinity. “I’m interested in the coordinated movements of ants and other species,” he explains. Having observed the movement of ants, he creates simulations and programs that predict how ants might move based on theories. Menon plays a short clip on his phone of one of the theoretical models created by him and his team. The video on the screen shows the generated image of a haphazard mass of ants, and soon, amid their jittery random movements, a pattern seems to emerge and the ants form a circle where each is following the one before it. “This is a really interesting phenomenon that we have seen in nature. When there are large crowds of ants, eventually they’ll all just start moving in a circle, and often they just don’t know how to break that pattern of movement. Some can even die of fatigue,” Menon elaborates.

Where Sumana’s research makes observations based on experiments, Menon’s research looks at the real-life patterns of ants and then tries to understand and mimic them by creating mathematical explanations and theories for those movements. To test out some theories concerning the coordinated movement of ants, a PhD student at IMSc, Ananta Dutta, is considering setting up a laboratory to conduct experiments. Dutta hopes to test the theories on actual ants and maybe even create an ant map of the IMSc campus.

“I have gone to Sumana’s lab and seen her experiments with tandem running, but when we look at the same subject from the theoretical perspective, our questions are different,” she explains. Dutta elaborates that research shows that about 20% of an ant colony leads the tandem runs. “The funny part is that 50% of the colony knows how to do tandem runs,” Datta says, “So as a theoretical scientist, we ask why that 20%? How have ants arrived at that optimization, and does it apply to other species also?”

Beyond tandem running, ant relocation also raises questions about the process of relocation itself. Colonies often tend to relocate when their nest is damaged, or if there is a lack of resources around, or even when they grow too large and splitting could benefit them. This process can be theoretically analysed by looking at a mathematical model known as ‘the minority game’ which analyses the behaviour of a group.

Menon illustrates the minority game with an analogy. If a man is deciding whether to go to a bar or not, he has two options–going or staying home. The result of these two choices leads to four potential outcomes: If he goes to the bar and it is crowded then he would regret not having stayed home; if he stays home and the bar is not crowded then he would wish he had gone. However, if he stays home and the bar is crowded then he is happy to have avoided the crowd, and if he goes to the bar and finds it empty, then too he can enjoy his choice. “Winning the minority game is about benefiting from being in the minority,” Menon says, “We don’t know how, but ants seem to solve this problem collectively without any explicit coordination.”

Whether ants are relocating or simply deciding which path to take, their choices reflect an efficiency which benefits the larger community. J. Hareesh, another PhD student at the IMSc observes, “All this makes us wonder whether ants have a collective intelligence, and how they manage to be so efficient without much effort.” Such observations lead to new theoretical models to describe their behaviour, more theories, and more experiments. However, the general public might wonder what purpose such research serves, and what practical results it leads to.

While movements of ants have been abstracted into algorithms and used to create efficient delivery systems, Sumana’s research falls under what she calls ‘Blue sky Research’. She elaborates, “You are curious, you need an answer, you need to understand something. That’s it. It may not have any implications.” Not necessarily driven by a clear goal or an aim, such research is based on allowing scientists to pursue their curiosity. Often however, even though research might begin without an aim it leads to great discoveries just as Alexander Fleming's casual hobby of growing bacteria cultures in his laboratory led to the discovery of antibiotics. “It was not because somebody said there is an antibiotic to be discovered,” says Sumana.

There might be a lot humans can learn from observing ants regarding optimization, but Hareesh, believes that reportage of ants tends to look at them from a human perspective and anthropomorphise their behaviour. “Nature can only tell you what is, it cannot tell you what ought to be,” says Hareesh, borrowing an idea from the philosopher David Hume. Sumana agrees and asserts, “We come from a sociological, cultural background and we should absolutely not apply that thinking to any other organism.”

“All our stories while growing up are about hard working ants, but are they actually hardworking?” Sumana asks, explaining that often in a colony many ants are not doing much most of the time. When we look at their societies from a sociological perspective, translate their physical differences and roles into ‘castes’, or think that they form a kind of ideal community, “We are making the mistake of anthropomorphising ants and approaching them with our own morals and ethics,” she warns.

Hareesh thinks that people often look at ant species with the idea that ‘we must be like that’. He elaborates, “You can pick a convenient species, a convenient behaviour, and then say we should be like that, but even within ants you will find contradictory behaviour too.” While some ant species are known for their hard working caretakers and intricate social hierarchies, in others workers violently kill the queen when issues arise. There are also some species where the queen bites off the wings of all females, but as she ages and fails to do so, a generation of females with wings is born again and proceeds to fight for dominance by biting at each other's wings. “Is there intention behind such behaviour? Probably not. That is just how different species have evolved,” says Hareesh.

Research about ants, whether it be theoretical or experimental, is not a search for concrete results that can be applied to human societies. The intention is to understand these complex organisms, and to Sumana, such ‘curiosity for the sake of curiosity’ is an essential aspect of scientific inquiry. Althought naturalists, theoreticians, and experimentalists perceive ants differently, they are intruigued by the same fundamental questions. How are such high levels of efficiency acheived? How do individuals come together to enhance the performance of the collective? What kind of an individual and collective intelligence do ants possess? What governs their division of labour? How much autonomy does an individual ant really have?

Sumana’s research into ants began when she first noticed the phenomenon of tandem running. “In my optimism, I thought a few years would be enough to understand this and solve it,” she recalls. She began with the idea of relocation and then launched herself into other related questions about the cognition and communication of ants. “Now, 15 years and eight PhD students later, there are so many more questions waiting to be answered!” Sumana exclaims, “We are just knocking at the door,” she says, convinced that there are enough questions to keep her occupied for atleast another two decades.
