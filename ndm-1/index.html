NDM-1: A tourist and a threat

The year was 2008, Barack Obama had been elected the 44th president of the United States, hundreds of tibetan monks had gathered in Lhasa to protest Chinese rule, terrorist attacks had swept through Mumbai, Beijing had hosted the summer olympics, and the ripples of the financial crisis were making their way through global economies. In the outskirts of Sweden, a 59 year old man was hospitalised. He had recently returned from New Delhi. His urine tests revealed the presence of a bacteria that contained a devilish little protein known as NDM-1.

Two years later, three patients in the US were found to be infected with NDM-1. Each of them had travelled to receive health care in India. Soon, NDM-1 cases began to pop up across the globe, all connected to people who had travelled to India or Pakistan for cosmetic surgeries and organ transplants. In 2011, Turkey, Spain, Ireland, and the Czech Republic reported cases of NDM-1, and by 2012, medical tourists had carried NDM-1 to 29 countries of the world.

In September of 2010, the Lancet Journal of Infectious Diseases wrote, “The potential of NDM-1 to be a worldwide public health problem is great, and co-ordinated international surveillance is needed.” The danger might have been just a minuscule fragment of DNA, but it held the potential to shatter global health systems.

As NDM-1 began its voyage through the bacterial kingdom, it gave bacteria the power to resist a majority of antibiotics. If this phenomenon were to become more prevalent, humanity would have to battle untreatable infections of all kinds. This came at a time when misuse of antibiotics among humans, animals, and plants had already led to the rise of antimicrobial resistance. Furthermore, as the medical tourism industry grew, it brought along unforeseen challenges which are still being ignored by many. Unlike humans, NDM-1 and other invisible threats of its kind, need no passports or visas. Today, they travel around us, among us, within us, with no fixed origin or destination. Aided by medical tourism, exacerbating the crisis of antimicrobial resistance, the threat of NDM-1 is the first of its kind. The scientific and medical community have yet to devise their battle strategy against it.

The story of NDM-1

NDM-1, New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase-1, was named after the city where it was discovered. Lurking around in New Delhi since 2006, it had gone unnoticed for two years. Scientists took another two years to study and recognise the danger it posed. NDM-1 is a fragment of DNA called a plasmid which can easily spread among bacteria. It is dangerous because when it finds its way into a bacteria, it allows it to resist fourteen classes of antibiotics including powerful intravenous (IV) antibiotics used as a last resort when patients fail to respond to anything else. On its own, NDM-1 is harmless, but if it were to find its way into a bacterial pathogen, that particular strain would become almost untreatable.

NDM-1 is a type of carbapenemase, an enzyme that is produced by certain bacteria, which can make antibiotics like carbapenes, penicillins, and cephalosporins ineffective. A sub group of carbapenemase known as MBLs or metallo-beta-lactames can break down major groups of drugs. These enzymes use zinc ions to break apart the antibiotics, and their complex structure makes them hard to target with medicines. Scientists have not managed to create effective drugs that can block the MBL enzymes because their active sites, where the reactions take place, are complicated and vary from enzyme to enzyme.

So far, humans have been lucky. NDM-1 has mostly been found in bacterial species that live harmlessly in the human body. Klebsiella pneumoniae inhabit healthy people’s mouths, skin, and intestines, while Escherichia Coli live in our guts. The danger however lies in the potential for NDM-1 to transfer itself into a dangerous bacterial pathogen, causing a health crisis where diseases stop responding to antibiotics.

Before the discovery of antibiotics in 1928, a large number of people died regularly from infections which are now curable. However, if plasmids like NDM-1 spread across the bacterial kingdom, the World Health Organisation fears “the doomsday scenario of a world without antibiotics.” Transplants, cosmetic surgeries and treatments that involve suppressing the immune system, or even simple surgical procedures could lead to untreatable infections that prove to be fatal.

Antibiotics have a fairly short shelf-life and drug companies have little incentive to invest in developing news ones. The market value of a new antibiotic is about 50 billion dollars, an insignificant amount when compared to the resources a drug company would have to spend on its research and development. In 2009, according to the infectious diseases society of America, only 16 of the new drugs in development were antibiotics, and none of them targeted the most resistant and least treatable bacteria like those with NDM-1.

For now, modern medicine has only two drugs that can treat NDM-1 infections. The first is an old antibiotic that fell out of use in the 1980s after it was found to be toxic. The second is an expensive IV antibiotic called tigecycline which is approved only for soft tissue infections on the skin, muscles, and connective tissues.

Bacteria containing NDM-1 have developed a new and dangerous kind of antimicrobial resistance. Durai Balaji, a surgeon at the General Medicine department at Apollo Hospital in Chennai, admits, “I read an article yesterday that said 50 thousand child deaths were caused by antimicrobial resistance last year. We should definitely take this seriously.” Surrounded by crowds of international patients who frequent Apollo hospitals across India, Balaji shook his head, “The international patients only ask for antibiotics, never about antimicrobial resistance.” Yet, when asked whether he saw a link between medical tourism and antimicrobial resistance, Balaji frowned, “There is no link. I can’t think of any dangers in medical tourism. We have a great scope in this area.”

Apollo: mythical sun god to medical tourist hub

An ambulance whizzes past, a fleeting blur of red and white against a backdrop of Bengali restaurants. It is 7 AM. The rest of Chennai is waking from its slumber, but Greams Lane never even went to sleep. How could it, when a 24/7 Apollo Emergency Centre lies at the end of one of its little alleys.

As the main road breaks into smaller arteries, a sign reads, ‘Apollo Hospitals’. Beneath that, in bright red capital letters, it says ‘Emergency and Trauma Care’. The torrential flow of traffic turns into a trickle of wakers and bikers. Along the red walls, a large advertisement says ‘Sri Krishna Ayurvedic Spa Healthcentre’ with pictures of relaxed looking women. To the right, the ‘Rain Drops’ hotel looms over the road, more than half a dozen floors tall, its tinted glass windows reflecting the sky. For 13 years the building has stood there, the first and only initiative of the Kerala Tourism Development Corporation (KTDC) to exist outside Kerala. “We have 90 rooms,” says TM Sandeep, the manager, “usually they are occupied by Bangladeshis, but sometimes we also get people from the US, UK.”

Along the lane, the smell of hot puris escapes a little roadside restaurant. Standing along the footpath, a short man in a kurta pyjama ushers his family away from the road. “Don’t block, don’t block, come here, come here, hospital is this side,” he says in Bengali, chiding a young girl, two middle-aged women, and an old woman, all dressed in floral prints. “8 for 20!” Shouts a roadside vendor in Bengali, carrying a mobile stall of pani-puri around his neck.

Down the road, the entrance to the Apollo main block is a large rectangular archway. A line of autos stands outside along the wall. Cars go past, some just passing by, others entering the gateway towards the valet parking desk. A Mercedes slows down at the entrance, slowly glides up the slope, and pauses before the valet desk. A man in khaki coloured pants and a white shirt waits by the door, as a woman in dark blue scrubs emerges out of the car. As she scurries off towards the Apollo main building, the valet drives off to park her car. Behind them, in the open waiting room, an old man in a dhoti sleeps on the soft brown sofas. To the right, large red letters read ‘Emergency 24/7’.

Around the entrance of the Apollo main block, there are three kinds of people. The staff, those who scurry around, walking with urgency, garbed in a variety of blues, whites, greens, and pinks, dressed to reflect their occupation and seniority. Then there are those who wait, sometimes sitting on their suitcases, sometimes holding a folder of documents and bills, sometimes sitting on the steps, leaning against the wall, eyes half closed. Finally, there are the patients, with their guardians hovering around them, and the Apollo staff navigating their wheelchairs.

To the right, past the Emergency Centre and the ‘North East Help Desk’ lies the ‘International Patient Desk’, a hall with a dozen chairs, two large tables, and a bas-relief of the world map in what resembles wood. On the left most corner, below the logo of Apollo is a framed certificate that reads ‘Tamil Nadu Tourism Awards 2023’. A tall woman in a burkha walks in, her heels click-clacking on the granite floor. Behind her, a short man follows, holding the hands of a little girl in a bright yellow kurta, and a boy in a white kurta pyjama and skull cap. After a brief conversation with the person at the desk, the woman spins around and begins making her way to the admissions office. The urgency of her gait makes the fabric of her black burkha flutter. As she swings her arms, a little badge pinned to her shoulder, swings along. It is a miniature flag of Bangladesh.

“We get patients from across the world, but mostly they come from Bangladesh, Mauritius, Saudi Arabia, sometimes Europe and the US,” says Abdul Aziz from the International Patients Admission Office, a thin moustache drooping over his lips. “Every day we admit about 10 to 15 patients from Bangladesh. Once or twice a month we get someone from the US,” he says.

The air-conditioned lobby of the Apollo main block hardly has that peculiar smell that is often associated with hospitals. Just outside the sliding glass doors is a line of wheelchairs, and a man in a green shirt waits beside them. He is a patient guide at Apollo. His job is to scan the crowds and identify people who might need assistance finding their way around. Waiting with him is Anub Shah, who has been a translator at Apollo for 14 years. “There are doctor desks all over the hospital, and we have about four to five doctors per desk, and one translator is assigned to each desk,” he says. He too confirms that the majority of patients come from Bangladesh. His phone rings, and after a brief two-minute conversation, he says, “There is a patient from Mauritius who just got admitted in Teynapet. The doctor will go to do the surgery now. I’ll have to go too.” Saying that he walks off towards the main block, surrounded by Bengali chatter, as the lobby resembles a busy street in Kolkata.

Apollo on Greams Lane, Chennai, is a prime destination for medical tourists. The quality of healthcare coupled with the relatively cheap cost of treatment draws international tourists to its compound. However, despite the large posters celebrating ‘World Antimicrobial Resistance Week’ many fail to make the connection between antimicrobial resistance and medical tourism. Gautam Kumar, a surgeon in the emergency department says, “Patients don't follow proper antimicrobial guidelines, but by coming to Apollo they are getting the right treatment.” He believes that patients get mismanaged in their own countries, and coming to Apollo is a way of correcting those wrongs. “We are getting closer to the kind of care given in the US and the UK. Everyone is following universal guidelines. No one is prescribing medicines just like that.” Reacting to the name of NDM-1 he exclaims, “We follow evidence based medicine. Nothing like that can happen at Apollo.”

The blurry boundaries between treatment and travel

Medical tourism might look like a modern phenomenon, and perhaps its current state has been enabled by industrialisation and globalisation, but it has existed since antiquity. Epidaurus, a city in Greece, was known for its healing sanctuary, the Sanctuary of Asclepius, where the greeks travelled to be cured of their illnesses. To those ancient medical tourists, the sanctuary offered a theatre, a stadium, a gymnasium, baths, a hospital, and places for the patients and their guardians to sleep. However, today’s medical tourism has taken on new, larger dimensions, bringing along with it its own set of risks.

Patients are attracted to countries like India and Thailand that offer cheap and good quality health care. Travel agencies that cater to this particular kind of tourism have mushroomed around private hospitals, offering care-packages that include sightseeing and other local adventures along with the medical treatment. Such agencies often act as facilitators in organising travel plans, accommodation, and even post-operative care. Through medical tourism, patients can save money, access unavailable or uninsured treatment, avoid the long wait time for procedures, combine medical care and tourism, as well as maintain discretion for private treatments like plastic surgery.

However medical risks to such activities are inevitable. Epidemiological conditions vary in different countries, so patients can often ferry diseases to and fro between countries. Complications can also arise when procedures require patients to stay on longer in a particular country, or when there are post-operative requirements after they have returned home.

Along with medical risks, medical tourism also poses legal and ethical risks. International laws differ and since there is no single set of global health regulations, some countries might have limited consequences for medical errors. Some countries might even lack provisions where patients can seek out legal claims or insurance in cases of medical malpractice. Medical tourists also risk being duped and exploited by travel agencies which might not provide them with enough information about their treatment and stay.

For countries like India that have a booming private healthcare system, medical tourists spur on economic growth. Host countries benefit by investing in private healthcare infrastructure, services, and medical education. Tourism revenue sees a welcome boost, and medical professionals stay or return to such countries in the presence of better facilities and opportunities. The Indian government even introduced the M Visa (medical visa) to make health-related travel easier. However, when resources are directed towards private healthcare, it is often at the cost of public healthcare.

Medical tourism often caters to affluent foreign patients and wealthy locals, creating a dual healthcare system where public healthcare is underfunded and understaffed, leading to inequality in the accessibility of healthcare. Skilled professionals, nurses, and doctors, prefer high paying jobs in private healthcare when compared to public healthcare.

Furthermore, the focus of healthcare itself shifts from the country’s epidemiological priorities to high-profit treatments for non-urgent conditions. Issues like malnutrition, communicable diseases, non-communicable diseases, maternal or infant mortality cease to be priorities. Primary healthcare is given less importance compared to secondary or tertiary healthcare. For the less privileged, medical treatments push them into poverty and sometimes crushing debt. According to the National Sample Survey 2000, illness-related debt made up 40% of total debt in India. However, some of the invisible dangers to medical tourism are less talked about.

When the Lancet first published a report on the dangers of NDM-1, the Indian Government was quick to deny its health significance. Advocates of medical tourism began saying that the report had been an attempt to demean the Indian medical industry. The Indian Express published an article that claimed this was “a conspiracy to hurt Indian medical tourism.” Now, almost 15 years later, numerous research papers have established a link between antimicrobial resistance and medical tourism.

The increased exposure to new microbes, the carrying and ferrying around of resistant strains of bacteria, and the healthcare challenges that crop up, are all realities of today’s medical industry. As we celebrate “World Antimicrobial Awareness Week” (WAAW) from November 18th to 24th, it must be acknowledged that antimicrobial resistance is a hot topic among researchers and yet medical tourists are often unaware of what it really entails.

When the bacteria fight back

The man-microbe conflict is fraught with many little victories and defeats. Each side returns with fortified artillery only to realise that they might win the battle but the war persists. The discovery of antibiotics is often considered a revolution because it taught humans to finally defeat previously untreatable infections. Soon however, microbes began to evolve too, often thanks to human error and negligence.

In recent times, germs have developed numerous techniques to thwart antibiotics. Sometimes they limit the access of the antibiotic by changing or reducing the number of ways to enter their cells. For example, some types of bacteria form an outer membrane that prevents antibiotics from entering. Germs can also use pumps in their cell walls to expel antibiotics that have managed to enter the cell. Then there are germs which possess enzymes like NDM-1 which can alter or break down antibiotics. The knowledge to defeat antibiotics lies in the DNA of the germs, and luckily for them, they can easily exchange DNA and share resistance mechanisms even with germs which have never been exposed to antibiotics or antifungals.

So far, very few techniques exist to fight off antimicrobial resistance, and many of them are too complex and expensive to replicate on a large scale if necessary. Human monoclonal antibodies, which are lab-made proteins that mimic human antibodies, have been used in special cases. Trials to develop and test monoclonal antibodies are ongoing. Meanwhile, antibody-antibiotic conjugates, which combine monoclonal antibodies with potent antibiotics have also been used to deliver antimicrobial treatment. Bacteriophages, viruses that target specific bacteria, also show potential in the man-microbe war, but phage therapy brings challenges of its own. Bacteriophages need to be purified before being used, and they need to be specific to the bacteria at hand. Furthermore, phages can also lead bacteria to become more powerful and resistant to survive bacteriophage attacks.

The WHO has proclaimed antimicrobial resistance to be one of the top global public health and development threats. Based on WHO estimates, in 2019, antimicrobial resistance was directly responsible for 1.27 million global deaths and indirectly responsible for 4.95 million deaths. The WHO blames the misuse of antibiotics among humans, animals, and plants for the emerging drug-resistant pathogens.

Antimicrobial resistance is a complex problem that demands a multifaceted response. The ‘One Health’ approach to understanding wellness asserts that the health of humans, animals, plants, and the environment are not only linked but interdependent. In fact, one of the biggest reasons for the spread of antimicrobial resistance is the overuse of antibiotics in agriculture. In 2017, 73% of all antibiotics used were given to animals. In a world where zoonotic diseases are growing prevalent, such practices might prove to be extremely costly in the future. It is evident that to control the threat of antimicrobial resistance we have to change our attitude in handling antimicrobial medicines for humans, animals, and plants. In the midst of this, the emergence of NDM-1 highlights what could go terribly wrong if antimicrobial resistance was allowed to flourish unchecked.

The way forward

As of now, NDM-1 has not caused a large-scale disruption in the fabric of global healthcare. However, someday it could. Is it too late to stop that day from coming? We do not know. What we know is that people consume far too many antibiotics in an unregulated manner. Some take too few doses of antibiotics, allowing bacteria to develop immunity to them, and some take too much of antibiotics, which leads to the same result. People pop in antibiotic pills even for illnesses which do not require them. Furthermore, as medical tourists grow numerous, the need for regulations of all kinds becomes even more urgent. Whether it be in the case of prescribing medicines, allowing risky treatment, or even conducting proper check-ups before and after treating patients, medical tourism requires global healthcare guidelines.

If NDM-1 and its likes are to be fought off successfully, further studies must be conducted to better understand the nature of antimicrobial resistance and the kind of weapons we could manufacture against it. For now, however, the thousands of Greams Lanes, and Apollos of the world will continue being hosts to medical tourists. Bacteria will continue travelling visa-free across the globe. NDM-1 too will continue silently pervading the bacterial kingdom.
